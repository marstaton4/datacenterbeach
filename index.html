The short version

Annex first, classify later. That’s backwards.

Prime Storage’s sister company, Edge Inference, wants to convert 20,000–25,000 sq ft of the storage building at 610 US-17 BUS into what it calls an “inference point.” The Town’s own FAQ says the only item before Council now is annexing 2.3 acres into the town. But the same FAQ says the Planning Director decides what the use is only after annexation.

Council would be voting on the land before anyone has formally decided what is going on it.

All we’re asking: pause, get it in writing, then decide.

Scale

“Small” depends on where you plug it in.

Yes, hyperscale campuses draw 100 to 1,000 MW, and that’s the comparison the FAQ leans on. But Surfside Beach is a small beach town, not Northern Virginia. The right yardstick is the town’s own grid, not Google’s.

Translate it into homes

At an average continuous draw of about 1.2 kW per American home (a rough rule of thumb), each square below is ~100 homes.

2.5 MW ≈ 2,000 homes20 MW ≈ 16,000 homes

The cap is 8× the starting load, and 16,000 homes’ worth of electricity is far more than a town of a few thousand residents uses. All of it would sit behind a Waffle House on Highway 17 Business, beside homes and the town’s main commercial corridor.

The label

Why this is a data center.

THE FAQ SAYS
“Not a data center.”
  • Doesn’t “store” data
  • Goes inside an existing building
  • Works “like a telephone exchange”
THE SAME FAQ ALSO DESCRIBES
A data center.
  • Racks of compute, 24/7, at 2.5–20 MW
  • A closed-loop liquid cooling system
  • Possible backup generators and electrical/fire review
  • Part of a national network of “edge” sites
01

“Doesn’t store data” isn’t the test

A data center is a building of servers doing computation. Processing instead of archiving doesn’t change the power, cooling, generators or footprint.

02

“Telephone exchange” is branding

An exchange doesn’t need a sealed coolant loop sized to last 30 years or a multi-megawatt power upgrade.

03

It’s one of hundreds

Local reporting on the company’s Virginia proposal says its network could reach over 3,500 MW across 347 sites in 33 states. Surfside could be one node in that.

04

Promises aren’t ordinances

The 20 MW cap and no-water pledge appear in an FAQ. Council hasn’t said they’ll be enforceable conditions of annexation or zoning.

05

Your own use classification is pending

The FAQ says Edge will ask the Planning Director to classify the use after annexation. Council should know the answer first.

06

The upside is unproven

Claims about 911 and hospital benefits are general. No agreements with Surfside Beach’s emergency services are cited.

Follow the money

The shell is for someone else.

In April 2026, Prime Group Holdings, the parent of Prime Storage, announced a nationwide rollout of “edge data centers” and battery storage at its self-storage properties, in collaboration with Microsoft and Hanwha. Microsoft’s cloud executive was quoted in the announcement saying the project shows how Microsoft Azure can support real-time AI at the edge.

Prime’s own press materials say “edge data centers.” Only the Surfside FAQ says it isn’t one.
THE MODEL

Powered shells

Prime says its edge is leveraging existing power capacity for a faster “time-to-power.” In industry terms, that’s a building with power and cooling ready for a tenant’s hardware. It appears the tenant could be a hyperscaler, not a local 911 center.

THE PRECEDENT

Marietta, GA: 18 MW

City staff in Marietta describe a planned 18 MW Prime Storage data center conversion there, with most storage units demolished to make room for support infrastructure. Surfside’s “never above 20 MW” isn’t far off.

THE PITCH

“Inference point”

Local officials elsewhere are being given the same term. Virginia coverage notes it is, by definition, a data center that runs trained AI models.

Sources: Business Wire (Prime Group, Apr. 21, 2026); Data Center Dynamics; Blackridge Research; WHRO/WVTF; datacentermap.com. Whether Azure workloads would run in Surfside has not been stated publicly. We’re asking Council to ask.

It already happened next door (in Georgia)

Marietta, GA: same company, same pitch.

Prime Storage’s Powers Ferry Place site in Marietta, Georgia, is the closest public paper trail to what is proposed in Surfside. The city’s own staff report describes an 18 MW data center inside an existing self-storage building, with most exterior storage rows demolished for an equipment yard. The Town can read the same documents we did.

THE SAME PITCH

“Low latency” for traffic and hospitals

Prime’s Marietta application justifies the use with traffic controls and medical facilities, nearly the same 911/hospital argument in Surfside’s FAQ.

NO SPECS

“Closed-loop air-cooled”

Staff noted air-cooled systems save water but need high-velocity fans, and that no specifications were provided to verify how they’d be designed or operated.

NO RULES

Code didn’t define a data center

Marietta staff said the zoning code had no data-center use, size distinction, or equipment, noise or screening standards. Ask: does Surfside’s code?

NOISE

They measure bass, too

Marietta’s ordinance limits residential noise to 65 dBA / 84 dBC by day and 60 dBA / 79 dBC at night. The C-weighted figure is the one that catches low-frequency hum. Ask Surfside for dBC limits.

THE MIX-UP

How big is it, really?

The applicant’s narrative says about 40% of the building would be converted, then later says about 22%. Surfside should demand one clear, signed number.

THE LAWYERS

Constitutional challenge filed

The application included a constitutional challenge to the city’s zoning, which typically preserves the right to sue if denied. This is the legal posture a small town should expect.

Marietta’s lesson is hard to miss: staff flagged the gaps, the code had no standards, and the city approved first. Nearby jurisdictions the staff report names, including Cobb County, Atlanta, Athens-Clarke and Clayton County, adopted moratoriums or new standards, and Marietta itself soon moved toward a pause. Surfside can do the sensible order of operations: write the rules, then decide.

What Marietta finally required

A Marietta council member who voted yes published a report listing the conditions Council attached to the approval, including Prime’s own Aug. 11 stipulation letter. Whatever you think of the vote, it shows what a town can demand, and what Surfside’s FAQ does not promise.

NOISE

56 dBA / 77 dBC, always

Hard limits with no emergency exception, continuous monitoring at the residential property line, and an independent study within three months of completion. Prime had offered only annual reporting; Council changed it.

GENERATORS

Nine 2.5 MW diesel generators

Prime’s sound study models nine Cummins 2,500 kW diesel generators, about 22.5 MW of standby power. Prime’s letter caps scheduled testing at 150 combined hours a year, daytime only, one generator at a time. Startup, commissioning and real emergencies are not counted. Council added continuous emissions monitoring. Prime also committed to renewable diesel.

HEAT

Independent heat study

A computational heat-dispersion study is required after installation, against temperature-rise limits near the equipment yard and out to 300 feet.

LOCK-IN

Site-specific, under 50%

The data use is limited to under half the largest building and tied to the approved plans. Council had to expressly bar conversion of two other storage sites. Prime’s own letter says capability may grow.

THE CATCH

Marietta owns its power company

The report counts about $4.2M a year in utility profit, part of roughly $7.5M in estimated annual revenue, because Marietta runs its own electric utility. Surfside’s FAQ points to Santee Cooper, so don’t assume the same payoff here.

THE GAP

Surfside has promises, not terms

The FAQ describes none of this as enforceable: dBA-only noise limits, no continuous monitoring, no generator-hour cap, no independent studies, no site lock.

Inside Prime’s sound study

Prime’s consultant, Bowman Consulting, filed a 10-page sound study with Marietta. It is the first look at the actual equipment behind one of these projects, and the first public answer to “will there be generators?”

THE EQUIPMENT

The exact models

Generators: 9 × Cummins 2500DQKAN diesel (QSK60 engine, 2,500 kW standby each), in GPC GWE-G3-2500-3960 Level 3 enclosures guaranteed at 75 dBA at 7 m.
Chillers: 9 × YORK YVAA0456 air-cooled (8 running, 1 spare), 106 dBA each before silencers.
Transformers: 9 × 3,000 kVA.
A 20-ft barrier encloses the equipment yard and a 10-ft barrier runs along the neighbors. Equipment may be substituted later. If Surfside’s project is similar, expect equipment on this scale. Ask.

THE LABEL

“Data Center Campus”

The study is titled “GA10 Data Center Campus” and calls the project a data center facility. It also describes a two-story building, while the city staff report describes a one-story one. Ask which is right.

MODELED

Predicted, not measured

The silencers and generator enclosures hadn’t been bought yet. The study assumes their performance as minimum requirements, to be confirmed later. The only real measurement was two days of ambient sound near the neighbors.

THE RESULT

47–49 dBA predicted

Worst-case modeled levels at the neighbors: 47 dBA in normal operation and 49 dBA with one generator testing (57 and 68 dBC). Existing night ambient there was 55 dBA, mostly from I-75.

LOOSE LIMIT?

56 dBA vs. 49 predicted

The council member’s report says the final 56 dBA / 77 dBC limits match the study’s modeled levels. The study’s maxima are lower, so the limits leave roughly 7–9 dBA and 9–20 dBC of headroom. Possibly to allow for ambient noise, but worth asking.

SURFSIDE’S LIMIT

50 decibels overnight

Marietta’s modeled worst case of 49 dBA is within 1 dB of the Town’s overnight limit next to homes. Also ask whether Surfside’s noise code exempts emergency generator operation, as Marietta’s does.

Bottom line: strong protections are achievable, but Marietta got them by demanding them in writing, before the final approval. Surfside can do the same.

Reality check

What “up to 20 MW” really means.

NOISE

Hard to measure, easy to hear

Cooling plant and generators produce low-frequency hum that carries through walls and ground. The FAQ’s limits are decibel readings at the property line, but low-frequency noise barely registers on a standard dB(A) meter. Who will test it, how often, and who pays?

GENERATORS

Generators: “if used”

The FAQ says only that if generators are used, they would run in outages and for “short routine maintenance tests.” The FAQ gives no count, size, fuel type or testing schedule, and doesn’t say whether there will be any. Ask for all of it, plus whether an air permit is needed.

POWER

“No rate hike” isn’t “no impact”

Even if the project pays for its own upgrades, 20 MW running around the clock is real generation somewhere. Santee Cooper’s mix includes coal and natural gas, so new constant load means more fuel burned. That’s not a bill line item, but it is a cost to the air we breathe on the coast.

MONEY

Around $225 million

Per a Marietta council member’s report, Prime projects about $225 million of investment for its 18 MW project there. At up to 20 MW, expect a similar order of magnitude: serious money, and serious legal firepower on the other side.

David vs. Goliath

A small town vs. a very large legal team.

A 2.3-acre annexation is a modest petition. The project behind it may be anything but. Prime and its partners can field land-use counsel and engineers who do this daily across 30+ states. A town of a few thousand cannot match that on its own, and verbal assurances in an FAQ won’t help once the land is annexed and the rezoning approved.

So before the vote: hire independent land-use counsel and a qualified acoustical engineer for the Town; require a written development agreement with an enforceable MW cap, noise testing (including low-frequency), generator hour limits, emissions reporting, decommissioning and removal terms, and penalties paid by the company, not residents. If they mean what the FAQ says, they’ll sign it.

What we’re asking

Six asks. All reasonable.

  1. Delay any final action on the annexation. Nothing is lost by getting this right.
  2. Get the use classified in writing first, with Edge’s full description of power, cooling, generators, staffing and traffic made public before any vote.
  3. Hold a public hearing on the actual use, not only the 2.3-acre boundary line.
  4. Commission independent review of electrical load, noise, fire safety, hurricane/flood risk and utility impacts.
  5. Retain independent counsel and an acoustical engineer for the Town, paid for by the applicant.
  6. Make every promise binding: MW cap, no municipal water, no new structures, generator hours and noise limits written into ordinance or development agreement.
